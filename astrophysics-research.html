Galactic fountains, disk accretion, and chemical evolution

For other researchers

Can outflows from nuclear star formation drive gas accretion and shape the star formation and metallicity profiles of galactic disks?

Gas launched by a circumnuclear starburst can fall back onto a galaxy's outer disk. Because this gas carries less specific angular momentum than the disk material it joins, its return can drive radial inflow while supplying metals and fuel for star formation.

Working with Todd Thompson, I develop models connecting nuclear star formation to accretion, star formation, and chemical enrichment across galactic disks. I combine analytic and numerical calculations with observed gas, star formation, and metallicity profiles of nearby spiral galaxies to explore when this fountain driven picture is physically consistent.

Proto-neutron star winds

For other researchers

I use relativistic magnetohydrodynamic simulations in PLUTO to investigate outflows from proto-neutron stars. By varying the stellar mass, thermal conditions, magnetic field, and rotation, I examine how these ingredients affect the development of a sustained wind.

The analysis follows the evolving mass and energy fluxes and the flow's critical points, connecting the numerical solutions to the physics of wind launching and acceleration.

ASAS-SN variability searches

For other researchers

At Ohio State, I lead a search for stellar dimming events, gravitational microlensing, and long term variability using ASAS-SN (All Sky Automated Survey for Supernovae). This network of telescopes continuously monitors the entire sky, measuring how bright millions of stars appear over time. These measurements create what astronomers call "light curves": graphs showing how a star's brightness changes over days, months, and years.

Long lasting dimming can reveal dust clouds moving across our view of a star. Gravitational microlensing produces a temporary brightening when the gravity of a foreground object magnifies light from a background star. Studying these events alongside long term and periodic changes in stellar brightness helps distinguish dust obscuration and lensing from variability caused by stellar activity.

I built MALCA (Multi timescale ASAS-SN Light Curve Analysis), a parallel processing pipeline designed to analyze more than 17 million ASAS-SN light curves. It uses Gaussian process models to describe each star's ordinary variability and systematic trends, then Bayesian comparisons to identify significant dimmings and brightenings relative to that baseline.

I inject synthetic events into real light curves to measure detection efficiency as a function of event amplitude, duration, and source brightness. I combine light curve properties with catalog crossmatches, infrared colors, and machine learning to classify candidates and distinguish microlensing, dusty dimming, and other kinds of variability.

This work is being conducted in collaboration with Christopher Kochanek and Krzysztof Stanek at Ohio State. The code is available on GitHub.

ASAS-SN survey operations and transient vetting

As a Research Technician I in Ohio State's Department of Astronomy, I perform daily image quality control for 20 cameras on five ASAS-SN telescopes, remotely recalibrate telescopes, and verify supernova and other transient candidates. I help release confirmed discoveries to the public, connecting the survey's observations to reliable transient reports.

Examples include the Transient Name Server discovery reports for ASASSN-26df (AT 2026lru) and ASASSN-26dr (AT 2026osq), which credit Krzysztof Stanek and me on behalf of the ASAS-SN team.

Cross-correlation spectroscopy

For other researchers

In my first author study of the ultra hot Jupiter KELT-20b, I used high resolution PEPSI/LBT transmission spectra to follow neutral and ionized iron absorption through a transit. Their different velocity patterns and transit asymmetries probe atmospheric winds and structure, while comparisons between studies show how adopted system parameters affect the inferred velocities.

I built a processing pipeline to remove absorption from Earth's atmosphere and contamination from the host star, then cross correlate the remaining spectra with atmospheric models generated using petitRADTRANS. By injecting synthetic planetary signals into the data and measuring how well the pipeline recovers them, I test how the processing affects the atmospheric signal.

This work grew out of my undergraduate research with Marshall Johnson and Ji Wang. I presented an early analysis as a poster at the 243rd American Astronomical Society meeting in January 2024 and defended my undergraduate thesis in December 2024. The study is published as PIRANGA II in the Astronomical Journal; the preprint is also available.

For the general public

Ultra hot Jupiters are an exceptional class of exoplanets. They rapidly orbit their host stars in close proximity and have puffy atmospheres that reach more than 2,200 Kelvin. They are expected to be tidally locked, meaning one hemisphere permanently faces the star, causing a large temperature gradient across the planet. With high quality observations, we can study the extreme physical processes governing these atmospheres. We observed one such exoplanet, KELT-20b, with the Potsdam Echelle Polarimetric and Spectroscopic Instrument (PEPSI) on the Large Binocular Telescope (LBT) located in Mt. Graham, Arizona.

To examine the atmosphere, we collect the light from the host star that passes through the exoplanet's atmosphere during a transit, some of which is absorbed by constituent elements in the atmosphere. Due to the quantum nature of electrons, each atom and molecule only absorbs photons at characteristic wavelengths, which allows us to identify the atmospheric constituents by examining the wavelengths where light is absorbed. Typically, exoplanet researchers take many exposures over the course of a transit, then combine their data to create a transmission spectrum that shows how much light is absorbed at each wavelength. Instead of measuring the transmission spectrum exclusively, it is often advantageous to calculate the similarity between the observed spectrum and a computer generated spectrum with the cross correlation method.

By analyzing these absorption features in detail, we can not only pinpoint the chemical makeup of the exoplanet's atmosphere but also probe the underlying physical processes. We can infer temperature gradients, wind speeds, and circulation patterns. These inferences are strengthened through comparison to theoretical models.

As the hot Jupiter transits, light from the star passes through different slices of the atmosphere, meaning that our transmission spectrum should evolve over time. As mentioned, observers commonly combine all exposures during transit together to maximize signal strength. In addition to the full transit spectrum, we examine how the absorption signatures change with orbital phase. This means that we can probe individual longitudinal slices of the atmosphere, gaining a considerably more detailed view of the atmospheric physics.
